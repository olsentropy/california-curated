I’m always keeping an eye out for new California books. Books can be a treasure trove for new ideas to write about, new ways to see the world around you. I read a lot of non-fiction, and I constantly find small details buried in a chapter here and there, or encounter the work of a scientist I’ve never heard of, and voila, I’ve got a new story to research.

Which is why it’s so sad we all [seem to be doing less reading](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/2026/08/reading-crisis-postliterate-age/687618/).

A while back, I made a list of what I consider [the top ten science and nature books](https://californiacurated.substack.com/p/ten-essential-books-about-californias) written over the decades about the state, but I recently noticed a surge in new books about California and got really excited about many of them. I even bought a few. In fact, right now I am reading Joseph Ogilvy’s _[Tin Can Coast: A History of Industry, Greed, and Fishing in the Golden State](https://link.amazon/B078x2KjF)_, which discusses the unfathomable historical abundance of marine life off the coast and how, in just a few generations, so much of it was nearly wiped out. But [things are coming back](https://californiacurated.substack.com/p/california-gave-the-ocean-room-to)!

I should add here that two independent publishers publish exceptional books about California’s natural world and history: [Heyday](https://www.heydaybooks.com/) and [Backcountry Press](https://backcountrypress.com/). Each has a wonderful catalogue of books covering a wide range of interests, from plant life to lizards and frogs to indigenous culture and the overall ecology of the state. _These publishers deserve our support._ They are small, but deeply committed to the intellectual value and art form of the book. I highly recommend you check out their Websites.

And so, these are ten recent and forthcoming books that have caught my attention. Other than _[Tin Can Coast](https://link.amazon/B078x2KjF)_, I haven’t read any of them. I am just flagging them for you to check out for yourself.

Hey, maybe we do a California Curated book club? 🤔

**[California Inside Out: Mapping an Ecology of Mind](https://link.amazon/B05j2qq4P)**

By Obi Kaufmann

If you are unfamiliar with Obi Kaufmann, you should change that. Kaufmann has spent years water painting, mapping, and thinking about California’s forests, deserts, and coast. We mentioned his trilogy among our favorite California books. In this new volume, he examines how language, assumptions, and habits shape how we perceive the land and influences what we’re willing to protect. His watercolors are always stunning. I think he’s one of the great author/artists alive today, so I am excited to get my hands on this one. I enjoy how he compels us to understand California through its living systems, all of which are intertwined.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4VAj!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc9fb09b7-98a0-4cf6-a8ff-af5b7c721dd7_1339x2048.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B078x2KjF)

**[Tin Can Coast: A History of Industry, Greed, and Fishing in the Golden State](https://link.amazon/B078x2KjF)**

By Joseph Ogilvy

Abalone, otters, sardines, and tuna are the backbone of this history of California’s coast. Extraction has always been a part of the California story, and too often we have taken from the land and sea to the point of collapse. We’re still seeing the effects of overfishing off our cost today. Ogilvy writes about the people and industries that turned an extraordinary marine abundance into fortunes and canned goods, all the while depleting fisheries to near disastrous results. He gets into the social aspects of all this, too, discussing immigration, racism, and the rise and fall of Cannery Row in Monterey. The book can be a bit of a downer, but its also a part of the state’s history that echoes into the modern day.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!FJ0g!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Feb914a34-9511-4398-92a3-00bbf3e74c51_1365x2048.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B0cGk6SvN)

**[The Telephone: A New History](https://link.amazon/B0cGk6SvN)**

By James Gleick

This one reaches a bit beyond California, but I’m making room for it. I’ve read several of Gleick’s books ([Genius: The Life and Science of Richard Feynman](https://link.amazon/B053xPvtT) is excellent), and I consider him one of the best non-fiction writers out there today. Gleick follows the telephone from an unlikely invention into a device that changed work, friendship, business, and the mundane experience of conversation. California has a place in the story, with early amplifier research done in Palo Alto as well as the opening of transcontinental telephone service to San Francisco. I’ll pick it up just because I like Gleick, but I’m also interested in how technology transforms the world for the better.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!iNRw!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F44b96670-2e00-4f48-88c0-477970b4d8a0_1341x2048.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B05mqeLjd)

**[California Dreams: The Making and Remaking of the University of California](https://link.amazon/B05mqeLjd)**

By Miriam Pawel

California is a [Nobel science powerhouse](https://californiacurated.substack.com/p/california-is-a-nobel-powerhouse). The University of California system alone counts 75 Nobel Prizes among its faculty and researchers, putting it at or near the top of all 50 states. I’ve learned writing this newsletter that if you spend any time reporting on California science, you will find your way back to the University of California. Pawel’s expansive book traces the University of California’s growth through its political battles and struggles over access, while bringing to life graduates whose achievements reflect the system’s extraordinary breadth. It’s a history of a university system that helped shape the state, but also the world. University systems all over the planet try to imitate the UC’s success.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8Hge!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F251938b5-abbf-402a-9d2b-319c1fbe88a2_723x1080.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B06li55wn)

**[Golden State: The Making of California](https://link.amazon/B06li55wn)**

By Michael Hiltzik

This is more history than science or nature, but you can’t really talk about one without the other. Hiltzik tries to explain how California became California, from Spanish colonization and the Gold Rush to agriculture, mining, oil, Hollywood, and Silicon Valley. The California dream is a story of ambition, often reckless; of stunning failure and cataclysm; and of rebirth and reinvention. Even today, perhaps especially today, California is still grappling with issues of dispossession and exclusion, as well as gnarly battles over wealth and power. I expect I’ll take on this book at some point, though several other California history books, including Kevin Starr’s, are waiting on my shelf. I mention Pawel’s book because it looks like a valuable new addition to the genre.

**[Firestorm: The Great Los Angeles Fires and America’s New Age of Disaster](https://link.amazon/B03X4svhV)**

By Jacob Soboroff

This is one I have in audiobook format, but have not listened to yet. But it is very timely and important given the issues we’ve had with wildfire over the last decade or so. Soboroff covered the January 2025 Los Angeles fires as both a reporter and someone who watched his hometown burn. He reports from the ground after the Palisades and Eaton fires, capturing wildfire’s human toll and how a single spark can change lives in an instant. For those of us in Southern California, issues of wildfire are a near daily concern. Even though the answers are sometimes obvious, it’s sometimes worth asking how we become so vulnerable, and what does it take to be better prepared?

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eTZd!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdcb9d59a-da94-42e6-9ea5-3ca843d568dc_1356x2048.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B0cIurjCU)

**[Yosemite Falls: Reckoning with California History and the Gospel of John Muir](https://link.amazon/B0cIurjCU)**

By Daniel Duane

For Duane’s family, Yosemite was sacred ground, and John Muir helped wrote the scripture. This is a memoir of rock climbing and family life that also delves into the history behind the devotion to the outdoors. There’s a darker side to the story, of course. There almost always is. There’s that all to common question: what happens when you love a place, but then learn things that complicate the story you grew up with?

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!paHl!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6cef3c61-5fc2-422d-9811-80a64d29c6a7_518x800.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B016iPgRZ)

**[The Mysterious World of the Bull Kelp Forest](https://link.amazon/B016iPgRZ)**

By Josie Iselin, illustrated by Ellen Litwiller

As a diver, I love kelp forests. It’s heartbreaking to watch California’s bull kelp disappear. Just offshore, they are the foundation of a hidden world, an entire ecosystem of creatures. Iselin and Litwiller tell the story of bull kelp and its impact on California’s off-shore environment and all the critters that make California so special: abalone, rockfish, urchins, sea stars, otters, all an important part of the North Pacific coast. I’m looking forward to this one. It sounds fascinating, and the illustrations look beautiful.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!CNsL!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F28667985-03a6-48d0-b468-714d727c6dcf_1366x2048.jpeg)](https://link.amazon/B07qTzk9q)

**[Deep Time in the Mono Lake Basin: Nature and History over the Last 10,000 Years](https://link.amazon/B07qTzk9q)**

By Robert B. Marks

I’ve probably been to Mono Lake 100 times. I walk the shore, step through clouds of alkali flies, and marvel at the stunning, otherworldly tufa towers. But the basin has a history that reaches beyond the salty shoreline. Marks delves into the region’s ecology, the history of the Kootzaduka’a people, hydroelectric development, and the diversion of water to Los Angeles that almost ruined the lake. Then he follows the effort to repair the damage. I’ve written here about Mono Lake, and this looks like a chance to gain a better understanding of the people and decisions that shaped it.

**[The Rush: California Gold, the Civil War, and the Making of the Modern World](https://link.amazon/B08HJyHPP)**

By Nathaniel Philbrick

Nathaniel Philbrick has always struck me as a quintessential East Coast writer. Almost all his books are set there, and he lives on Nantucket. So it will be interesting to see how he tackles a West Coast story as colorful and calamitous as the California Gold Rush. The Gold Rush is so familiar that it’s easy to forget how strange and consequential it was. Philbrick is one of those historians, like David McCullough, who wouldn’t take on a book this broad in scope without a trove of original sources. The Gold Rush has inspired dozens of books.

He traces the upheaval from the mining camps and San Francisco waterfront to the fight over slavery and California’s place in the Union. Remember, gold was discovered just days, literally _nine_ days, before the United States and Mexico signed the treaty that ceded California. Its impact is still being felt today. I’ll read Philbrick’s book because I respect his work, and I’m sure I’ll learn something new.

That’s it! I hope you get a few of them for your own enjoyment and fulfillment. And remember to support Heyday and Backcountry.
