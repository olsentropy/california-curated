Half Dome, El Capitan, Mount Whitney and pretty much every granite wall [Ansel Adams](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansel_Adams>) ever photographed are pieces of the same enormous thing. It's called the [Sierra Nevada Batholith](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierra_Nevada_Batholith>), a body of granite so big it basically is the Sierra Nevada. It's what's left of the roots of an ancient chain of volcanoes, cooked deep underground and then slowly brought up into the light.

John McPhee described it this way: "It lies inside the Sierra like a big zeppelin. Geologists in their field boots mapping outcrops may not have been able to find a bottom, but geophysicists can, or think they can, and they say it is six miles down. If so, the batholith weighs a quadrillion tons, and its volume is at least a hundred and fifty thousand cubic miles."

The granite formed miles underground during the [Mesozoic era](<https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/mesozoic-era.htm#:~:text=The%20Mesozoic%20Era%20\(251.9%20to,ruled%20the%20land%20and%20air.>), between about 85 and 220 million years ago, when molten rock rose into the crust and cooled there without ever erupting.

That's what a [batholith](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batholith>) is: a huge mass of "intrusive" igneous rock, mostly granite. (Intrusive doesn't mean the rock is rude. It means it pushed into other rock underground and cooled there instead of erupting from a volcano.) The Sierra Nevada Batholith covers about 16,000 square miles, which makes it one of the biggest anywhere.

So how did it get there? For more than 100 million years, an ocean plate called the Farallon Plate was sliding under the edge of North America. As it sank, it melted the rock above it, and that magma rose into the crust. Some of it erupted from a line of volcanoes, like the Andes today. Most of it stalled underground and cooled very slowly, slowly enough for big crystals to grow, which is why Sierra granite looks like a speckled kitchen countertop.

Then, over millions of years, miles of rock on top of it eroded away, and the granite was exposed. Today it runs from just north of Lake Tahoe to [Tehachapi Pass](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tehachapi_Pass>). Picture an air mattress 450 miles long, 60 miles wide and eight or nine miles thick, with about six miles of it still underground and two or three miles sticking up as the mountains.

It isn't one solid chunk, though. The batholith is made of many separate blobs of magma, called [plutons](<https://www.britannica.com/science/pluton-igneous-rock>), that rose at different times and fused together, packed in like cobblestones. In places you can see the rounded tops of individual plutons, some 10 by 20 miles across. Each one has a slightly different chemistry. Granite is 60 to 80 percent silica. The light parts are quartz and feldspar, the black specks are minerals like biotite and hornblende, and sometimes there's a reddish tint from iron oxide. The slower it cooled, the bigger the crystals.

One of the biggest and youngest is the Whitney Intrusive Suite, which makes up the tallest mountain in the U.S. outside Alaska, [Mt. Whitney](<https://californiacurated.com/2023/04/23/the-man-for-whom-mt-whitney-was-named/>).

![](https://californiacurated.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/batholith-map.png?w=800)A geologic map of Yosemite National Park showing the many intrusions that make up this part of the Sierra Nevada Batholith. 

Wind, water and ice did the uncovering, a little bit at a time over millions of years.

Glaciers did the most dramatic work. During the ice ages, rivers of ice ground through the granite and carved places like Yosemite Valley, with its sheer walls and rounded domes.

![](https://californiacurated.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/yosemite-half-dome.jpg?w=1024)Half Dome in Yosemite, a piece of the Sierra Nevada Batholith. (Erik Olsen)

The granite matters for more than the scenery. Water doesn't soak into it easily, so snowmelt runs off it into streams, rivers and reservoirs instead. That runoff supplies much of the water for California's farms and cities, which means a lot of the state is, in a sense, drinking off the batholith.

If you want to go deeper, read [Kim Stanley Robinson](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Stanley_Robinson>)'s _[The High Sierra: A Love Story](<https://www.amazon.com/High-Sierra-Love-Story/dp/031659301X>)_. Robinson is one of the best science fiction writers alive, but he's also been hiking the Sierra for decades, and this book is part memoir, part natural history and part love letter to the range.

![](https://californiacurated.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/half-dome-olsen.jpg?w=1024)Half Dome: ancient magma that cooled underground and was later uncovered by uplift and erosion. (Erik Olsen)

He [mixes his own trips](<https://www.theatlantic.com/books/archive/2022/07/the-high-sierra-kim-stanley-robinson-book-review/670587/>) with the geology, the Indigenous history and the threats the mountains face now. It's great. (If you haven't yet read one of his books, you should. Start with [Red Mars](<https://www.amazon.com/Red-Mars-Trilogy-Stanley-Robinson/dp/0553560735>).) 

![](https://californiacurated.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/sierra.jpg?w=1024)Sierra Nevada from Lone Pine (Erik Olsen)

So the next time you're climbing in Yosemite, hiking near Tahoe or just looking up at the Sierra from Lone Pine, remember that you're looking at the frozen insides of a volcanic system that was running more than 85 million years ago.
